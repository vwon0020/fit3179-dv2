Who's in your
pocket?

Australians are among the most loyal iPhone users on Earth. This story maps the phones the world carries, then zooms in on Australia: the brands we pick, the screens we use, and what our money actually buys.

How the numbers are measured. Brand "share" in this story is the share of all web pages viewed from each brand's devices, as counted by StatCounter on more than 1.5 million websites. It shows which devices people actually use, not how many were sold that year. Hover over any chart to see exact values. Where a chart has a menu or a clickable legend, use it to highlight one group.

01

The world

A planet split between two brands

Across most of the world, the phone in your pocket is either an Apple or a Samsung. Which one depends heavily on where you live.

Money talks

iPhones cost more than most Android phones, so richer countries tend to have more of them.

The map below combines two measures: the iPhone share of phones in use (StatCounter) and GDP per person, which is the value a country produces each year divided by its population (World Bank). Dark plum countries, like Australia, are rich and iPhone-heavy. Teal countries are rich but prefer other brands. Pink countries choose iPhones despite lower incomes.

Look for: South Korea, home of Samsung, is rich but only 31% iPhone. National loyalty can beat income.

02

Australia

An iPhone nation

If you lined up 100 Australians and checked their phones, about 61 would be holding an iPhone. Worldwide, only about 31 would. Chinese brands, which make up about a quarter of phones in use worldwide, barely register here.

The podium rarely changes

Ranking brands by their average share each year shows how stable the top spots are. Apple has been No. 1 and Samsung No. 2 every year since 2012. The fight is for third place: HTC held it in 2012, Huawei in 2020 and Google in 2026.

03

Beyond the phone

From the desk to the pocket

In 2012, nine in ten web pages Australians viewed were on a desktop or laptop. Today phones and computers are neck and neck, and the brand loyalty we show for phones carries over to tablets and laptops.

A slow, seesawing takeover

Each square is one month. Blue means computers were ahead and orange means phones were ahead. White triangles mark the months when phones won.

The gap closed steadily until 2019. Phones first won in December 2019, then COVID-19 lockdowns and working from home pushed browsing back to laptops. Phones led again in most months from January to August 2025. In September 2026 the two were just 0.05 points apart.

04

Specs & prices

What does your money buy?

If Australians mostly buy the dearest brand, do they get better hardware? A catalogue of 752 phones launched between 2019 and 2025 suggests the answer is "not on paper".

The price ladder

Each box shows the middle half of a brand's launch prices. The white line is the median, the middle price when all models are lined up from cheapest to dearest. The whiskers reach the cheapest and dearest models.

The median Apple phone launched at US$999, against US$799 for Samsung, US$449 for Oppo and just US$199 for Infinix, the budget brand that leads in parts of Africa.

Prices are US launch prices, the only currency the dataset lists consistently. Australian prices are usually higher.

Batteries got bigger, fast

A violin plot is a box plot that shows its shape. The wider the orange shape, the more phones launched with that battery size. The black tick marks the median.

In 2019 only 5% of new models had a 5,000 mAh battery or larger (mAh measures how much charge a battery holds). By 2024, 79% did. This is one spec where the whole market moved together.

So what?

Three things to take away

1

Australia is an outlier

We use iPhones at twice the global rate. In this respect we look more like the USA and Japan than our Asian neighbours, where Chinese brands are strong.

2

Loyalty spans devices

Our Apple habit extends to tablets (around 80% iPads) and increasingly to computers, where Mac use has doubled since 2012.

3

We pay for the brand

On paper, Apple phones cost the most but rank near the bottom for battery and camera megapixels. Value for money means different things to different buyers.
